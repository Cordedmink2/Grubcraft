# The War Table, episode 2: What's been built
MIA: Welcome back to The War Table. I'm Mia.
SAM: I'm Sam. Last episode was the design. This one is the build: what exists today, how it got there, and what's being worked on right now. And the headline number is a bit absurd.
MIA: Go on.
SAM: The repository was created on the twelfth of October with nothing in it but a readme. Five days later there are over a hundred and thirty merged changes. Each one is a pull request, which is a bundle of changes that gets checked automatically, reviewed, and then merged into the main game.
MIA: And that's not one person typing fast. It's a team of Claude sessions working in parallel threads, with Mario directing.
SAM: Right. One coordinator session watches the whole project, and separate threads each own one area: netcode, AI, Conquest, art, maps and so on. Each thread builds in steps, one pull request per step, and merges its own work once the automatic checks are green. Every merge comes with a download link for the Mac build, so Mario can try it.
MIA: Let's go through it in order. Day one.
SAM: Day one was planning and foundations. First a full set of design documents, twenty of them now, covering factions, economy, combat, maps, networking, performance and a roadmap. The planning pass ended with twelve recommendations, including the custom Rust engine, and Mario approved all twelve. Then milestone zero: the engine itself, a terrain, a camera, and automatic builds for Mac, Windows and Linux.
MIA: And the memory measurement from that first build is a good story.
SAM: It is. The first build used about a hundred and ninety megabytes on Mario's M4 MacBook Pro. But about a hundred and twenty of that is Apple's own graphics system, which every Metal app pays before it draws anything. So the game itself was tiny. That measurement is why the memory budget was rethought, which we'll cover in the lessons episode.
MIA: Then milestone one, regiments on a field.
SAM: Eight steps, and most of them landed on the first two days. Step one, formations in the simulation: files, ranks, facing, hit points per soldier. Step two, drawing every soldier individually on screen. Step three, the controls: drag a box to select, right-drag to draw a battle line with the width and facing you want, and control groups. Step four, combat: melee, arrows, and the rule that hitting a flank or rear does more damage. Step five, morale: wavering, routing, rallying and shattering. Step six, pathfinding around ridges and rivers. Step seven, a sandbox where you can set up any battle, slow it down and tune unit stats live. And step eight, real art: animated soldier models, a Commander View where, past a certain zoom, the soldiers disappear and each regiment becomes a block in its team colour, like Total War's tactical map, and a credits screen.
MIA: A quick word on that pathfinding, because the approach is interesting.
SAM: The original plan was flow fields, which is a common technique for big crowds. What got built instead is a route over a graph of sectors and the gaps between them, so a regiment gets a list of waypoints. It's simpler, cheaper, and suits blocks of soldiers that move as one. And there's a thread working on it right now, which we'll come back to.
MIA: Day two brought the computer opponent.
SAM: The first battle AI landed with three levels, Recruit, Veteran and General. It reads the ground, it uses cliffs, hills and rivers, and there's an AI lens: press L and you can see what each computer army is planning. That's a debugging tool that turned out to be fun for players too. What's built so far is the battle side of the AI's mind: the Spymaster that tracks what it can see, the Marshal that runs the army, and the Captain that fights the battle. The economy, strategy and diplomacy parts come with Conquest and the living world.
MIA: And then the Warlord.
SAM: The Warlord landed a few days later. It guesses your plan from the way you march and rehearses its own about ninety seconds ahead in scratch copies of the battle. In testing it beats the General eight battles in ten. More recently the AI learned team games. In a six-player game, the computer reads who's actually coming for it, lets two other sides fight first, and then falls on them. A pair using that against the old AI wins sixty-one percent of three-way battles. It also learned to cross a river at a ford you aren't watching, or to pull back and tempt you across, which stopped two AIs staring at each other over a river forever.
MIA: Let's do multiplayer, because that's Mario's priority.
SAM: That was milestone two, built in seven steps on the fifteenth. Step one, host, join and play on the same Wi-Fi. Step two, lag that adapts, and the battle pauses while a dropped player reconnects. Step three, encryption, lobby passwords and short join codes. Step four, every machine builds the identical map. Step five, a points budget for Field Battles, two, four or eight thousand points, an Army screen to build your own force, and a deployment phase where you place regiments in your zone with the enemy hidden, then press Ready. Step six, every battle saved as a replay with a Replays screen, plus rejoining: if your game crashes, you start it again, rejoin in your old place and catch up at speed. And step seven, packaging: one zip per platform with a read-me.
MIA: And then Mario said he doesn't actually want to play on Wi-Fi.
SAM: Right, he plays over the internet with Tailscale. The netcode worked unchanged, because it was built to adapt to lag, so the fix was small: the lobby now shows a Tailscale join code that friends anywhere can type. And on the sixteenth, lobbies went to six players, with two new team colours, Amber and Jade, and three versus three or two versus two versus two battles.
MIA: There's a safety net in there that's worth explaining. Golden replays.
SAM: Yes. Because the game is deterministic, a replay of a battle should play out exactly the same forever. So the team recorded a few reference battles, called golden replays, including a huge one with over six thousand soldiers. Every change to the code is checked against them automatically. If a change accidentally alters how a battle plays out, the replay drifts and the check fails. And if a change is meant to alter battles, the change has to re-record them and bump a version number, so old and new versions of the game refuse to play together.
MIA: And that six thousand soldier battle doubles as a speed test.
SAM: It does. Even the slowest simulation steps take only about a third of a millisecond, using about five megabytes of memory for the battle itself. There's an automatic check that fails if a change makes it more than five percent slower.
MIA: Now the battle feel, because that's where a lot of the most visible work went.
SAM: A whole run of changes. Duels that read the numbers, so the killing blow comes from the soldier actually fighting that man. Weapon room: spears fight from two ranks, big swings need space, shields matter. Lines that push, so the losing side is driven back and a regiment pushed into a river or a cliff is pinned and loses heart. Ambushes in woods and tall grass. Champions duelling before the battle. And then the Mount and Blade ideas: brace, fall back, the fallen staying on the field, riding with the banner, and battle cries.
MIA: Give me a couple of the details that show how carefully this was tuned.
SAM: Two good ones. Charges used to happen too often, because two lines that parted and met again would count as a fresh charge. The fix was a run-up: a regiment only charges after about a second of marching. In a test battle, charges fell from one thousand two hundred and fifty-eight to two hundred and fifty-three. The other: melee lines used to brush apart and re-close in about nine fights out of ten. Now locked lines stay locked, and a fresh enemy arriving doesn't reset who's winning.
MIA: And brace is properly physical.
SAM: Press Z with spears or pikes, and a charge into their front stops dead, falls into disorder and gets the charge's weight turned back on it. A flank or rear charge still hits home, which is exactly the Total War lesson: spears beat cavalry from the front, not from behind.
MIA: Let's talk about the art, because that went from coloured boxes to final-quality models in a few days.
SAM: First the engine had to be upgraded. Smooth skinning, so soldiers bend at every joint with skeletons of up to sixty-three bones. Painted textures with team colours. Sun shadows and glow. Then four levels of detail per soldier, picked by how tall it shows on screen, plus texture compression, which shrank the model pack from five point three megabytes to two point three. And soldiers are now posed once per frame instead of twice, eight to nineteen percent faster drawing.
MIA: And then the models themselves.
SAM: Built by Claude in Blender, from scratch. The first was a Yari Ashigaru, a spearman. Then Mario set up a contest: one thread on Opus built the whole Ember Clans roster, fifteen models, and another on Sonnet built the Carrion Concord, fourteen models, and Mario compared them side by side.
MIA: What do the Ember models look like?
SAM: Some highlights. Yumi archers drawing a real bowstring, with the arrow appearing as they draw. Workers carrying baskets of glowing Grub. Mounted samurai, where the rider and horse share one skeleton. The Onmyoji, a mystic hero with a wheel of glowing talismans behind his shoulders that burns out when he falls. A battering ram under wet hides with a death animation where a wheel breaks off. And a war kite with a rider hanging under painted paper that drops fire pots and crashes.
MIA: And the verdict on the contest?
SAM: Mario chose Opus at medium effort for all future 3D models. Right now a thread is building the Choir's eleven models in crystal and glass. All eleven are built and the last four are being baked into game format. The Brood come after that. And there's a unit viewer on the main menu now, where you can turn any model around, zoom in and play its animations.
MIA: Now the big one. Conquest.
SAM: Milestone three, the base-building mode, being built in six steps, and it moved fast. Step one, buildings in the rules: armies walk around them and can knock them down, with rams hitting three times as hard. All forty-six buildings of the four factions are in the game data. Step two, the economy: workers walk to a seep, fill a load, carry it home and go back. Five workers on a nearby seep bring in about two hundred Grub a minute. Step three, construction, training queues, rally points and tech tiers.
MIA: And then it became playable.
SAM: Step four put it in the lobby on two hand-made maps, Ribcage Pass for two players and The Four Teeth for four, with a speed setting called Tempo. It added the top bar with resources, supply, upkeep and the clock, and a command grid: select a building to train or upgrade, select workers to open a build menu and place a building with a ghost that turns red where it can't go. Step five, in the last day, made it winnable, with Annihilation and the Titan's Heart, and added fog of war. Regiments see about thirty-two tiles around them, towers see furthest, and enemy buildings stay on your map once you've spotted them.
MIA: And a map checker that proves the maps are fair?
SAM: Yes, and it's one of my favourite pieces. It checks that every start has equal walking distances to seeps, veins and the Heart, and that the income curve is within three percent for every start. It even caught a creep camp sitting on top of a cliff wall in Ribcage Pass.
MIA: Which brings us to random maps.
SAM: Mario approved a plan, and it's mostly built. Field Battles can be on a random Pass, Ford, Hill or Open map, with a New map button and a short map code you can share with friends. Random Conquest maps come in three recipes: Teeth, with starts on high plateaus; Bowl, with the Heart on a raised tongue; and Fen, with starts on dry islands among pools. Each one is generated from a seed, checked for fairness, and rerolled if it fails. And campaign battles will be fought on maps made from their region of the Titan's body: crater lakes, cavern walls, bogs, deep forest.
MIA: Balance has had three passes too.
SAM: It has. Balance is measured by running hundreds of AI-versus-AI battles for every pairing of factions. After the latest pass, with woods on the map, every pairing wins between forty-nine and fifty-two percent, except Brood against Choir at forty-four, which is the one to watch.
MIA: And there was a whole-codebase review.
SAM: On the sixteenth, a review of the entire codebase found twenty-three ranked issues, and two threads have been working through them. One example: in a network game, another player could send sandbox-only commands, like changing unit stats for everyone. The host now ignores those. Another: who won used to be decided outside the simulation, so it's now decided by the game rules themselves, identical on every machine and in replays. Crash reports, safe saving and protection against damaged network data all came out of that review too.
MIA: So what's happening right now, today?
SAM: Five things. First, big living online maps, Mario's top priority. That thread has built a Land setting with Plains, Mixed, Wild or Random, tested four hundred and fifty generated maps, and is running its final checks before merging. Second, Conquest step six: a six-player Conquest map and a soak test to prove a long six-player game stays inside six gigabytes. Third, better pathfinding: regiments were getting stuck on corners and stepping into water at fords. The fix keeps a route wide enough for the whole block, narrows into a column through tight gaps and widens after, and makes regiments queue at crossings instead of shoving. Fourth, the Choir models. And fifth, the remaining review fixes.
MIA: And what's still on the list for later?
SAM: Building art with visible construction stages. The Brood models. Heroes in Conquest, and a Conquest AI. Two small boxes for Mario to tick: one run on a Windows PC, and a check that a three-thousand-soldier battle feels like Shogun and runs smoothly on a modest laptop. And after that, milestone four, The Reach Lives, with hamlets, mercenaries and a living world.
MIA: Five days. It's a lot.
SAM: It is. And the reason it holds together is the boring stuff: one step per change, automatic checks on every change, golden replays guarding the battle rules, and docs updated in the same change as the code.
MIA: Which is a nice lead-in to episode three: the lessons, including the things that went wrong.
SAM: See you there.
