# The War Table, episode 1: The design of Grubcraft
MIA: Welcome to The War Table, the podcast about building Grubcraft. I'm Mia.
SAM: And I'm Sam. This is episode one of three. Today is design: what the game is, and more importantly why each big decision went the way it did. Episode two is what's actually been built, and episode three is what went wrong and what we learned.
MIA: Let's start with the core idea, because everything else hangs off it.
SAM: The vision document puts it in one line: Warcraft Three's base building meets Shogun Total War's regiment battles. You gather, build and tech up like Warcraft. But your army is made of regiments, blocks of eight to twenty-four soldiers that you command as one unit. They have formations, facing, flanks, charges and morale. Heroes are the one exception: single, powerful characters who level up, in the Warcraft mould.
MIA: Why regiments, though? Warcraft works fine with individual units.
SAM: Two reasons, and the second is the clever one. The first is feel: regiments give you that commander-of-an-army feeling Mario asked for. The second is that they cut the clicking. Twelve regiments is twelve decisions. A Warcraft army of the same size might be two hundred units to manage. So the game can be deep about positioning and timing without turning into a contest of who clicks fastest.
MIA: And that ties into a design pillar the docs call the five minute test.
SAM: Right. Someone who's played a few strategy games should be doing something meaningful within five minutes and feel fluent within fifteen. And that's backed by hard rules, not good intentions. Two resources, one supply number and one faction meter on the screen, nothing else. At most twelve buttons on any command card, always in the same grid positions. Every unit has one signature thing it does, readable from its silhouette. Every number that matters is visible on a tooltip. And the defaults are smart: new workers go to work on their own, and archers skirmish by themselves.
MIA: Then the setting. Long ago, a god-sized creature fell from the sky and died. Its body became the land, called the Reach, and its slow rot and regrowth seeps a glowing, edible ooze called Grub. That's the main resource. The second is Marrow, which you mine from veins in the Titan's bones.
SAM: And the numbers on those matter. A fresh seep holds seventy-five hundred Grub. When it runs dry, it doesn't vanish. It becomes dregs and keeps yielding a quarter of its normal rate forever. A Marrow vein holds three thousand and doesn't come back, but it also leaves dregs.
MIA: Why the dregs rule?
SAM: Stalemates. In a lot of strategy games, a long match ends with everyone mined out and nobody able to afford an army. Dregs guarantee a trickle of income, so a sixty minute game can still end with a fight rather than a fizzle. Supply is capped at one hundred and fifty, with the same costs for every faction, from one for a worker to six for a monster.
MIA: Now the factions, which I think is the most ambitious part of the design. Every faction harvests the same two resources, but each does it in a completely different way.
SAM: The doc calls it four economies, four different games. And it matters because it changes what raiding and defending even mean.
MIA: Start with the Ember Clans.
SAM: Feudal Japan. Spear walls, samurai cavalry, fire rockets, giant war kites. Their economy is the classic one: workers carry Grub to granaries. Readable and approachable, and the weakness is obvious: hit their workers. Their faction meter is Honor, from zero to a hundred. They earn five Honor when an enemy regiment routs fighting theirs, ten for winning while outnumbered, and lose five when one of their own regiments runs.
MIA: And they spend it on Edicts.
SAM: Yes. Rice Tax turns twenty-five Honor into three hundred Grub. Forced March speeds an army up by thirty percent. Banner of the Ancestors, for thirty-five, means no regiment in an area can rout for twenty seconds. And at tier three, Ancestral Return summons a regiment of spectral samurai. The design reasoning is lovely: Honor makes the Clans want to take fair fights and win them. That's the spirit of Shogun, written into the economy. They also get fifteen percent more defence in a tight formation when standing still, and their spears can brace to double their defence against a charge. Best at holding a line, worst at chasing.
MIA: The Brood.
SAM: Insects. Their workers burrow into the ground and feed a living vein network. So raiders can't kill workers, they have to cut veins. Their meter is Larvae. Every regiment hatches from a larva, each hive holds up to three, and a new one spawns every twelve seconds. So the meter is both a production limit and a constant decision: another worker, or another warrior?
MIA: And their signature trick.
SAM: Metamorphosis in the field. Every tier one Brood regiment can evolve, on the battlefield, into one of two tier two forms. It cocoons for fifteen seconds, completely vulnerable, and comes out as the new form, keeping its veterancy. Skitterlings become either Spitters, which melt armour with acid, or Burrowers, which tunnel under the terrain and ambush from below. The point is you adapt your army to the fight you're actually in, not the one you guessed at five minutes ago.
MIA: The Choir.
SAM: The Choir is the one I find most interesting on paper. They're crystal beings and they have no gathering workers at all. They place pylons called Resonators near a seep, and the seep just produces. Resonators within fourteen tiles of each other link with a visible beam, and any three linked Resonators form a Chord, a glowing triangle on the ground. Every seep inside a Chord produces extra, from ten percent for a small triangle up to sixty percent for the largest.
MIA: So it's a geometry game.
SAM: Exactly, and the weakness is the geometry. Kill one Resonator and every Chord using it collapses. Bigger triangles pay more but are harder to defend. And the opponent can see the choice on the map, whether a Choir player is being greedy or safe. Their meter, Resonance, charges while Chords are intact and buys things like Phase Shift, which teleports regiments to any Resonator. And in combat they have Refraction: their beam weapons pass through their own crystal soldiers and split. A Prism Lancer firing past a Shardling regiment hits two targets. So you position your own army as lenses.
MIA: And the Carrion Concord.
SAM: Merchant necromancers. Their workers, Ledger Clerks, build and mine Marrow, but their Grub comes from caravans travelling between their Counting House and a trade post, usually a neutral Grubfolk hamlet. Income per trip scales with distance. Long routes are rich and dangerous, short routes are safe and poor. Caravans are big, slow and visible, so ambushing them is how you hurt the Concord.
MIA: And the macabre part.
SAM: Tithe. Every soldier that dies on any side, within twelve tiles of a Concord Reliquary, pays them one Tithe. And Reliquaries are cheap and can be built anywhere they have vision, including right next to two other players' fight. So the Concord literally profits from everyone else's wars. Tithe can raise corpses into a temporary undead regiment, or a permanent one that costs no supply but slowly decays away from a Reliquary. And their undead never rout. Instead they crumble. They can only be reinforced with Tithe, not Grub. So mixing reliable dead troops with fragile living crossbowmen is the Concord's puzzle.
MIA: Four factions, four kinds of threat. Raiding the Clans means hitting workers, the Brood means cutting veins, the Choir means breaking triangles, the Concord means ambushing caravans.
SAM: In a six-player free-for-all, that's genuinely four different problems on one map.
MIA: Which brings us to the modes. Conquest and Field Battle.
SAM: Conquest is the main mode: bases, workers, heroes, tech tiers, up to six players. You win one of two ways, and the lobby lets you pick. Annihilation: destroy an enemy's main building and they get ninety seconds to raise a new one, or they're out. Or the Titan's Heart: hold the god's heart for three minutes. The Heart only opens later, at minute twenty on the standard speed setting, and you need it with no enemy standing there.
MIA: What's the design point of the Heart?
SAM: It gives a player who's behind a target that isn't "destroy everyone", and it pulls armies into the middle of the map, where the big set-piece fights happen. Field Battle skips the base entirely: build an army to a points budget, deploy it, and fight one big Total War style battle.
MIA: And the scale of the maps is a firm call from Mario this week.
SAM: Very firm. Big and alive, Warcraft scale. Several minutes to march across, a mix of biomes, terrain features, resource areas for each faction, and neutral outposts and bases. Grubfolk hamlets near the seeps that trade with you, or remember that you burned them. Mercenaries who serve whoever pays. And diplomacy, relations that change over time, is planned into the AI and systems even though not all of it is built yet. After version one there's a campaign across the Reach, with seasons and the peoples of the world.
MIA: Let's do the engine. The team built its own instead of using Unity or Unreal. Why?
SAM: Two reasons, and both are design reasons, not just technical ones. The first is lean memory. The goal was a full six-player war, thousands of soldiers, in a tight memory budget. That budget has moved, which we'll talk about in episode three, but it's now six gigabytes for the whole game. A general-purpose engine spends a lot of that before you've drawn one soldier.
MIA: And the second reason is multiplayer.
SAM: Yes, and this decision shapes almost everything. Grubcraft uses what's called lockstep. Every player's computer runs the exact same battle simulation, twenty steps per second. The only thing sent over the internet is the orders. Move here, charge that, build this. Not the position of thousands of soldiers.
MIA: Like two chess players at separate boards, calling out moves.
SAM: Exactly. And it has huge benefits. Very little internet traffic, which matters with thousands of soldiers. Replays that are just the starting setup plus the list of orders, so they're kilobytes. But there's a catch. Every computer must get exactly the same answer, every step, forever. If a Mac and a Windows PC disagree about one soldier by a hair, the two games slowly drift apart into different battles.
MIA: So how do you guarantee that?
SAM: The simulation uses only whole numbers. Ordinary computer fractions, called floating point, can round very slightly differently on different chips. So the battle code bans them entirely, and there's a test that checks the rule. Even the maths for terrain uses a custom whole-number version of sine and square root. One host computer sequences everyone's orders, and the input delay adapts to the connection, up to about four hundred milliseconds for a laggy link.
MIA: Let's talk art direction, because that changed this week.
SAM: The original plan was stylised low poly, chosen to be cheap to render. Then Mario said individual soldier combat is vital. Total War style duels, per-soldier fight animations, idle sway, the feeling that an army is alive. And the final art must be high fidelity. So the target moved to stylised high detail, think Total War Warhammer Three or Warcraft Three Reforged.
MIA: With a look per faction.
SAM: The Clans are Sengoku lacquer with ember orange light. The Brood are hive lacquer with real insect anatomy and a lime glow. The Choir are cathedral glass, frosted cyan and violet with floating parts. The Concord are merchant macabre, with mint ghost light and amber lanterns.
MIA: What about the fights themselves? What actually happens when two regiments meet?
SAM: Formations first. Line, Tight, Loose and Shield wall. Spears fight from two ranks, so the second rank strikes over the first. Big blades need room to swing. A shield wall turns arrows but marches at half pace. Then inside the melee, each soldier duels the one in front: parries, staggers, knockdowns. The losing side is pushed back. Flank and rear attacks hit much harder. A charge needs a run-up. And lines that lock together stay locked, rather than sliding apart.
MIA: And morale decides most battles.
SAM: It does. Regiments waver, break, run and can rally, so you usually win by cracking a line, not killing every soldier. Before a battle, champions can duel between the lines, and the winner's army takes heart. Terrain matters: fords funnel armies, hills help, and regiments in woods or tall grass are invisible until an enemy comes close, which makes ambushes real.
MIA: And some of that came from studying Mount and Blade.
SAM: Yes. A research pass picked apart what makes Mount and Blade battles feel good, and Mario approved two batches of ideas. We'll cover what landed in episode two.
MIA: Last design topic: the computer opponent.
SAM: The key decision is separation. The rules of the game live in the simulation. The AI minds run on the host and send orders exactly like a human. They can only use what their side can see. There are four levels, Recruit, Veteran, General and Warlord. And the Warlord does something a real general wishes they could: it rehearses the battle in its head using the real game rules.
MIA: You said minds. Is it one big brain?
SAM: No, and this is one of the nicer bits of the AI design. A mind is a small chain of command, like a real army. A Sovereign decides what it wants, every few seconds. A Spymaster keeps track of what it knows and believes. A Steward runs the economy and production. An Envoy handles relations and deals. A Marshal moves armies toward objectives. And under the Marshal, a Captain fights each battle, two to four times a second, choosing formations, flanks and when to withdraw. Different parties switch different layers on. A Field Battle AI only needs the Spymaster, Marshal and Captain. A mercenary company gets a small Sovereign whose only goals are to stay paid and stay alive.
MIA: And the relations side?
SAM: There's a ledger. Every party holds three feelings toward every other: liking, trust, and fear, where fear is worked out from how much army you have nearby. Events move those numbers and then fade. Trading with a hamlet nudges liking up. Razing one costs a lot of liking and trust, takes half an hour to fade, and word spreads, so every other hamlet turns wary for about ten minutes. Break a contract with one mercenary company, and every company wants more money from you. Trust is lost in one stroke and rebuilt over many honoured deals.
MIA: That's a really human set of rules.
SAM: And it's all in a data file, so it can be tuned without touching code.
MIA: Back to the Warlord's rehearsals. How do they work?
SAM: It builds a scratch copy of the battle from what it believes is out there, and plays out its options. Wait for the enemy, march on them, or decide as it goes, combined with different battle plans. Because the simulation is so fast, a forty-regiment battle simulated twenty seconds ahead costs about twenty milliseconds. And because it only rehearses what it can see, a hidden reserve or an ambush still surprises it. So ambushes stay worth setting, even against the hardest AI.
MIA: That's a nice example of a technical choice, the fast whole-number simulation, paying off in game design.
SAM: That's really the theme of the whole design. Regiments cut clicks. Dregs prevent stalemates. Honor rewards fair fights. Lockstep makes replays tiny and lets the AI think ahead. The decisions support each other.
MIA: Next time: what's actually been built. Over a hundred and thirty merged changes in under a week, from an empty repository to six-player lobbies and Conquest with fog of war.
SAM: See you then.
